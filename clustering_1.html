What is Clustering?

Unsupervised vs. Supervised Learning

Supervised learning (classification/regression) learns from labeled examples $(x_i, y_i)$. Clustering is unsupervised learning: the algorithm receives only unlabeled feature vectors $D = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ and partitions $D$ into $k$ subsets $C_1, \dots, C_k$.

Core Optimization Objective

A good clustering simultaneously achieves two goals:

  • Maximize intra-cluster similarity (points inside the same cluster are close together).
  • Minimize inter-cluster similarity (points in different clusters are far apart).

Common Distance / Dissimilarity Metrics

Euclidean Distance ($L_2$)
$$d_2(x, y) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d} (x_i - y_i)^2}$$

Straight-line distance. Standard default for low-dimensional continuous data; sensitive to feature scaling and outliers.

Manhattan Distance ($L_1$)
$$d_1(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{d} |x_i - y_i|$$

Grid / taxicab distance. Less sensitive to outliers and degrades more slowly than $L_2$ in higher dimensions.

Cosine Distance
$$d_{\cos}(x, y) = 1 - \frac{x \cdot y}{\|x\|_2 \|y\|_2}$$

Measures angle between vectors, ignoring magnitude. Ideal for text documents (TF-IDF) and high-dimensional embeddings.

Customer Segmentation

Group shoppers by spend & frequency to tailor marketing to VIPs vs. bargain hunters.

Genomics & Biology

Flag transactions sitting far outside any tight normal cluster as potential fraud.

Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering (HAC)

HAC builds a nested hierarchy of clusters from the bottom up (agglomerative = gathering together). Unlike K-Means, it does not require choosing $K$ beforehand and is completely deterministic (no random starting centers).

The 4-Step Bottom-Up Algorithm

  1. Start with $n$ singleton clusters: $C_i = \{x_i\}$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$.
  2. Compute the $n \times n$ pairwise distance matrix between all clusters.
  3. Find the two closest clusters $(C_i, C_j)$ under the chosen linkage criterion and merge them into $C_{ij}$.
  4. Update the distance matrix and repeat Steps 3–4 until only $1$ giant cluster remains ($n-1$ total merges).

Reading & Cutting a Dendrogram

  • Leaves (bottom): Individual data points at height $0$.
  • Y-Axis (Merge Height): Dissimilarity at which two clusters fuse. Taller vertical jumps mean merging very dissimilar groups.
  • Horizontal Threshold Cut: Slicing across the tree at height $h$ yields $K$ clusters equal to the number of vertical branches crossed by the cut line.
  • Rule of Thumb for Best $K$: Cut across the longest uninterrupted vertical gap in the dendrogram.

Linkage Criteria Deep Dive

Once clusters contain multiple points, the linkage criterion defines how to measure the distance $D(A, B)$ between two clusters $A$ and $B$.

Single Linkage (Minimum / Nearest)

Pitfall: Chaining
$$D_{\text{single}}(A, B) = \min_{x \in A,\; y \in B} d(x, y)$$

Cluster Shape: Can detect elongated, non-spherical shapes (like Two Moons).
Pitfall: Chaining—a few noisy points between two distinct clusters can act as a bridge and prematurely fuse them into one long string.

Complete Linkage (Maximum / Farthest)

Pitfall: Outliers
$$D_{\text{complete}}(A, B) = \max_{x \in A,\; y \in B} d(x, y)$$

Cluster Shape: Tight, compact, roughly equal-diameter spherical clusters.
Pitfall: Sensitive to outliers (one distant point inflates the max distance) and tends to break apart large or elongated clusters.

Average Linkage (UPGMA)

Balanced Middle Ground
$$D_{\text{avg}}(A, B) = \frac{1}{|A|\,|B|} \sum_{x \in A} \sum_{y \in B} d(x, y)$$

Cluster Shape: Compromise between Single and Complete linkage.
Behavior: Much less prone to chaining than Single Linkage and less distorted by single outliers than Complete Linkage.

Ward's Method (Minimum Variance)

Requires Euclidean
$$\Delta \text{SSE}(A, B) = \frac{|A|\,|B|}{|A| + |B|} \|\mu_A - \mu_B\|_2^2$$

Cluster Shape: Clean, compact, evenly sized spherical clusters (similar objective to K-Means).
Behavior: Merges the pair that causes the smallest increase in total within-cluster sum of squares (variance).

Rand Index (RI) & Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)

Cluster IDs are arbitrary (swapping "Cluster 1" and "Cluster 2" gives the exact same partition), so standard classification accuracy fails. The Rand Index avoids the label-permutation problem by counting agreements across all $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ unordered pairs of points:

Raw Rand Index (RI)

  • $a$: Pairs in the same cluster in both true & predicted.
  • $b$: Pairs in different clusters in both true & predicted.
  • $c, d$: Pairs where the two clusterings disagree.
$$\text{RI} = \frac{a + b}{a + b + c + d} = \frac{a + b}{\binom{n}{2}} \in [0, 1]$$

The Catch: Even random assignments produce many diff-diff pairs ($b$), inflating raw RI well above $0$ (often $0.5\text{–}0.7$).

Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)

Corrects raw RI for agreement expected purely by chance:

$$\text{ARI} = \frac{\text{Index} - \mathbb{E}[\text{Index}]}{\max(\text{Index}) - \mathbb{E}[\text{Index}]}$$
  • $\text{ARI} = 1.0$: Perfect match (up to label renaming).
  • $\text{ARI} \approx 0.0$: No better than random guessing (or trivial $K=1$ / $K=n$ cut).
  • $\text{ARI} < 0.0$: Worse than random agreement.

High Dimensions & The Curse of Dimensionality

Why does clustering struggle with many parameters (features)?
As the number of dimensions $d$ grows, volume expands exponentially and data becomes extremely sparse. A phenomenon called distance concentration occurs: the distance to the nearest neighbor $d_{\min}$ and the farthest neighbor $d_{\max}$ converge toward the same value:
$$\lim_{d \to \infty} \frac{d_{\max} - d_{\min}}{d_{\min}} \to 0$$
When every pair of points looks almost equidistant, distance-based clustering (especially Euclidean $L_2$) loses the contrast needed to tell "close" from "far."

Is There a Hard Dimension Threshold?

There is no single universal cutoff—it depends on how many features are noisy vs. informative and whether the data lies on a low-dimensional manifold. However, as a practical rule of thumb, standard Euclidean clustering noticeably degrades once you pass ~10–20 noisy/independent dimensions, and fails severely in hundreds or thousands of raw dimensions.

How Clustering Recognizes Similarity in High-D

  • Switch Distance Metrics: Use Cosine Similarity (compares angle/direction, great for text & embeddings) or Manhattan ($L_1$) (more robust than $L_2$).
  • Dimensionality Reduction: Compress features first with PCA (linear) or UMAP / Autoencoders (nonlinear), then cluster in the compact space.
  • Feature Selection & Scaling: Drop irrelevant features and standardize (z-score) so large-scale variables don't drown out others.
  • Subspace Clustering: Search for clusters that exist only within subsets of dimensions (e.g., CLIQUE, PROCLUS).

Exam Concept Check (Click to Reveal Answer)

Because clustering is unsupervised, cluster labels are arbitrary permutations. If the algorithm puts all true "Group A" points into "Cluster 2" and all "Group B" points into "Cluster 1", the clustering is 100% perfect—yet naive accuracy comparing label numbers would score 0%. Metrics like the Rand Index and ARI solve this by evaluating pairs of points (whether they belong together or apart), making them invariant to label renaming.
HAC always starts from the exact same $n$ singleton points and greedily merges the closest pair according to a fixed distance matrix and linkage formula (barring exact distance ties, the tree is identical every time). K-Means starts by placing $K$ initial centroids randomly (or via randomized K-Means++) and iteratively descends to a local minimum of SSE, so different random seeds can converge to different clusterings.
Choose K-Medoids when: (1) your dataset contains extreme outliers (since K-Means averages points, outliers pull centroids off-center, whereas a medoid must be an actual data point); (2) you need a non-Euclidean distance metric (such as Manhattan, Cosine, or edit distance on strings where a "mean" doesn't even exist); or (3) you want each cluster's center to be an interpretable, real example from the dataset.
Single Linkage defines cluster distance as the minimum distance between any single pair of points across two clusters: $D(A,B) = \min_{x\in A, y\in B} d(x,y)$. Because it completely ignores all other points in both clusters, a small trail of noisy points between two otherwise distant clusters creates short local hops that fuse the two clusters into one long, stretched-out chain.
Due to the Curse of Dimensionality, squaring and summing differences across 100+ dimensions causes distance concentration: $\frac{d_{\max} - d_{\min}}{d_{\min}} \to 0$, meaning all points become nearly equidistant and nearest neighbors lose meaning. Fixes include: reducing dimensions first with PCA / UMAP, switching to Cosine similarity (measuring direction rather than magnitude) or $L_1$ Manhattan, removing noisy features, or using subspace clustering.
