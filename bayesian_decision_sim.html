The Mathematics of Rational Decisions

1. The Observation and The Posterior

In classification, we observe a feature vector $x$ (e.g., cell area, perimeter) and want to determine the true state of nature $\omega$. In our binary example:

  • $\omega_0$: Benign (Class 0)
  • $\omega_1$: Malignant (Class 1)

Using Bayes' Theorem, we calculate the posterior probability of each state given our observation $x$:

$$ P(\omega_i|x) = \frac{P(x|\omega_i) P(\omega_i)}{P(x)} $$

Where $P(x|\omega_i)$ is the class-conditional density (likelihood), and $P(\omega_i)$ is our prior belief.

2. Maximum Expected Utility (MEU) & Conditional Risk

Simply knowing the probability of a state is not enough to make a real-world decision. We must map our actions $a_i$ (predicting class $i$) to a consequence. We define a Loss function $L(a_i, \omega_j)$, representing the penalty incurred by predicting $i$ when the truth is $j$.

The Conditional Risk $R(a_i|x)$ is the expected loss of taking action $a_i$, computed by weighting the loss of every possible true state by the posterior probability of that state:

$$ R(a_i|x) = \sum_{j} L(a_i, \omega_j) P(\omega_j|x) $$

The Maximum Expected Utility principle dictates that a rational agent should select the action that minimizes this expected conditional risk. (Minimizing risk is identical to maximizing utility).

3. The Decision Boundary

Assuming correct predictions incur $0$ loss, the risk of our two actions simplifies to:

  • Risk of $a_0$ (Predict Benign): $R(a_0|x) = L_{FN} \cdot P(\omega_1|x)$
  • Risk of $a_1$ (Predict Malignant): $R(a_1|x) = L_{FP} \cdot P(\omega_0|x)$

We choose action $a_1$ (Predict Malignant) if $R(a_1|x) < R(a_0|x)$. By substituting Bayes' formula and canceling the common evidence term $P(x)$, we arrive at the optimal decision rule:

Predict Malignant if:

$$ L_{FP} \cdot P(x|\omega_0) P(\omega_0) < L_{FN} \cdot P(x|\omega_1) P(\omega_1) $$

Notice the interactive graph: When you increase the False Negative Cost ($L_{FN}$), the right side of the inequality grows. The mathematical surface representing the "Malignant" decision region expands outward, eagerly claiming territory to ensure risky false negatives are minimized, even if it causes a spike in harmless false positives.
