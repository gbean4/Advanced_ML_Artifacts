Understanding the Concepts

1. The Generalized Correlation Coefficient

Standard Pearson Correlation ($r$) is powerful, but it has a massive blind spot: it only detects linear relationships.

If you select the Quadratic (Parabola) dataset, you'll see Pearson correlation drops to nearly 0.00. A straight line of best fit for a parabola is flat, so Pearson assumes the variables are completely independent.

Mutual Information (MI), however, measures how much knowing $X$ reduces uncertainty about $Y$. For the parabola, knowing $X$ lets you perfectly predict $Y$. MI correctly identifies this high dependency, making it a "generalized" metric that detects linear, non-linear, and categorical relationships.

2. The Information Venn Diagram

Information Theory uses Entropy ($H$) to represent uncertainty. The Venn Diagram visualizes how variables share information:

  • H(X): Total uncertainty of variable X.
  • H(Y): Total uncertainty of variable Y.
  • I(X;Y) - The Overlap: The Mutual Information. This is the uncertainty that is eliminated by knowing the other variable.
  • H(X|Y) - The Non-Overlap: Conditional Entropy. The uncertainty remaining in X after you completely know Y.

Try dragging the slider: As variables become more dependent, their circles merge. The overlap $I(X;Y)$ grows, and the remaining independent uncertainty $H(X|Y)$ shrinks.

3. Conditional Mutual Information

Conditional MI—written as $I(X;Y|Z)$—asks: "How much new information does $X$ give me about $Y$, assuming I already know $Z$?"

This is the backbone of advanced Feature Selection in Machine Learning. Instead of just picking features that correlate highly with the target, Conditional MI helps you avoid redundant features. If two features provide the exact same information about the target, Conditional MI will recognize that adding the second feature yields near-zero novel information.
