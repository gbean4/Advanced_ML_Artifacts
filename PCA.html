Level 1

Core Intuition & Why PCA Is Useful

The Tilted 3D Pancake Analogy: Imagine a 3D cloud of points shaped like a flattened pancake tilted at an angle. In the original $(x, y, z)$ coordinates, all three axes are needed to describe where a point lies. PCA rotates the coordinate system to find a new set of orthogonal (perpendicular) axes:
  • PC1: Points along the longest stretch of the pancake (direction of maximum variance).
  • PC2: Perpendicular to PC1, pointing along the second-longest stretch.
  • PC3: Perpendicular to both, pointing along the thin thickness of the pancake.

Because PC3 barely varies, we drop it and project the 3D pancake onto the 2D plane spanned by PC1 and PC2 with minimal information loss.

1. Curse of Dimensionality

Reduces feature count $d$, curbing overfitting and dramatically speeding up downstream training and inference.

2. Decorrelation

Principal components are strictly uncorrelated ($\text{Cov}(z_i, z_j) = 0$ for $i \neq j$), eliminating multicollinearity in linear/logistic regression.

3. Denoising

Assumes high-variance directions capture true signal while low-variance tail directions capture random noise; dropping the tail filters noise.

4. Visualization & Compression

Projects high-dimensional data (like 784-D images) into 2D/3D for exploratory analysis, or compresses embeddings by storing only top-$k$ projections.

Level 2

First-Principles Math Derivation

Memorize this 5-step Lagrangian derivation—it is a staple ML final exam proof.

Step 0: Centering (and Standardizing)

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ be the data matrix ($n$ samples, $d$ features). Subtract the column mean $\mu_j$ from each feature so every column has mean $0$. If features have different physical units (e.g., meters vs. kilograms), also divide each column by its standard deviation so every feature has variance $1$.

Step 1: Projected Variance Objective

We seek a unit direction $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ($\|w\|_2 = 1$) such that projecting centered data $X$ onto $w$ gives scalar projections $z = Xw$ with maximum sample variance:

$$\text{Var}(Xw) = \frac{1}{n-1} (Xw)^T (Xw) = w^T \left(\frac{1}{n-1} X^T X\right) w = w^T \Sigma w$$

where $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} X^T X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the sample covariance matrix.

Step 2: Constrained Optimization & Lagrangian

Without a norm constraint, $w^T \Sigma w$ would blow up to $\infty$. Enforce $w^T w = 1$:

$$\max_{w} \; w^T \Sigma w \quad \text{subject to} \quad w^T w = 1$$

Form the Lagrangian with multiplier $\lambda$:

$$\mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = w^T \Sigma w - \lambda(w^T w - 1)$$

Step 3: Take Gradient & Set to Zero

Differentiating with respect to $w$ (using $\nabla_w (w^T A w) = 2Aw$ for symmetric $A$):

$$\nabla_w \mathcal{L} = 2\Sigma w - 2\lambda w = 0 \implies \Sigma w = \lambda w$$

This is the eigenvalue equation! Substituting $\Sigma w = \lambda w$ back into the objective gives $w^T \Sigma w = w^T (\lambda w) = \lambda (w^T w) = \lambda$.

Key Mathematical Takeaways

  • Eigenvectors $w_1, \dots, w_d$ are the principal components.
  • Eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ is literally the variance of the data along PC$i$.
  • Because $\Sigma$ is symmetric positive semi-definite (PSD), eigenvectors are guaranteed mutually orthogonal and eigenvalues are real and non-negative ($\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$).
  • To reduce $X$ to $k$ dimensions, stack the top $k$ eigenvectors into $W_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ and project: $Z = X W_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$.

Equivalence to Minimum Reconstruction Error

PCA can equivalently be derived by minimizing the squared Frobenius norm between $X$ and its rank-$k$ reconstruction $\hat{X} = Z W_k^T = X W_k W_k^T$:

$$\min_{W_k^T W_k = I_k} \|X - X W_k W_k^T\|_F^2$$

By orthogonality (Pythagorean theorem), $\|X\|_F^2 = \|X W_k\|_F^2 + \|X - X W_k W_k^T\|_F^2$. Since $\|X\|_F^2$ is fixed, maximizing projected variance is mathematically identical to minimizing reconstruction error (as you saw in Sandbox 1!).

Level 3

SVD & Computational Issues

In practice, libraries like scikit-learn almost never explicitly form $\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} X^T X$.

Why Forming $X^T X$ Fails: Condition Number Squaring

Explicitly computing $X^T X$ squares the condition number of your data matrix:

$$\kappa(X^T X) = \kappa(X)^2$$

In 64-bit floating-point arithmetic, squaring a large condition number wipes out precision for smaller eigenvalues and causes severe numerical instability whenever features are highly correlated.

The Fix: Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Factorize the centered data matrix $X$ directly via $X = U S V^T$ ($U, V$ orthogonal; $S$ diagonal with singular values $s_1 \ge s_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$):

$$\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} X^T X = \frac{1}{n-1}(V S^T U^T)(U S V^T) = V \left(\frac{S^2}{n-1}\right) V^T$$
  • Columns of $V$ are the principal components (eigenvectors).
  • Eigenvalues are $\lambda_i = \frac{s_i^2}{n-1}$.
  • Projected data is simply $Z = X V = U S$.

Time, Memory & Data Regimes

Regime The Bottleneck / Issue Practical Solution
High-Dimensional ($d \gg n$) Forming $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ takes $O(d^2)$ memory and $O(d^3)$ time (e.g., $d = 100{,}000$ genomics features). Use Thin SVD in $O(n^2 d)$ time, or eigendecompose the $n \times n$ Gram matrix $X X^T$ instead.
Massive Datasets (Large $n$ & $d$) Exact SVD costs $O(\min(n^2 d, n d^2))$ and requires the full matrix in RAM. Randomized SVD (Halko et al.) approximates top-$k$ in $O(n d \log k)$, or Incremental PCA (mini-batches).
Sparse Data (e.g., TF-IDF) Centering $X - \mu$ converts zeros into non-zeros, destroying sparsity and exploding RAM. Use Truncated SVD (LSA) without centering, operating directly on sparse matrices.
Extreme Outliers Variance squares distances, so a single extreme outlier can hijack PC1. Use Robust PCA (decomposing $X = L + S$ into low-rank + sparse outlier matrices) or $L_1$-norm variants.
Model Selection

5 Ways to Choose Latent Dimensions ($k$)

Ordered from simple heuristics to statistically rigorous methods.

Method 1

Cumulative Explained Variance Threshold

$$\text{EVR}(k) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j}$$

Pick the smallest $k$ crossing a target threshold (commonly 90%, 95%, or 99%). Intuitive, though the cutoff is arbitrary.

Method 2

Scree Plot (Elbow Method)

Plot $\lambda_i$ against component index $i$ and find the visual "elbow" where the steep drop flattens out. The steep slope captures structured signal; the flat tail represents noise.

Method 3 • Best for Supervised ML

Downstream Cross-Validation

Treat $k$ as a hyperparameter for your classifier or regressor. Always fit PCA inside each CV fold (never on the whole dataset first!) to prevent data leakage, and choose $k$ minimizing validation error.

Method 4

Kaiser Criterion (Standardized Only)

When using the correlation matrix (standardized features, each with variance $1$), keep components with $\lambda_i > 1$ (explaining more variance than a single original variable). Caveat: often overestimates $k$ in high dimensions.

Method 5 • Statistically Rigorous

Gavish-Donoho Optimal Hard Threshold / Parallel Analysis

Separates true signal from white noise by comparing observed eigenvalues against the theoretical Marchenko-Pastur distribution (random matrix noise floor) or eigenvalues from randomly permuted copies of your dataset (Parallel Analysis), retaining only components that significantly beat the noise floor.

Final Exam Pitfalls Checklist

1.
Forgetting to Center $X$: If you skip subtracting the mean, PC1 points from the origin toward the data centroid instead of the direction of maximum variance.
2.
Unstandardized Mixed Units: PCA is not scale-invariant. A feature measured in millimeters will have $10^6\times$ the variance of one in meters and dominate PC1 unless standardized.
3.
Data Leakage in Cross-Validation: Fitting PCA on train+test (or before CV splitting) leaks validation distribution statistics into training. Always fit on train folds only, then transform val/test.
4.
Assuming Linearity: PCA only finds linear subspaces. If data lies on a curved manifold (e.g., a Swiss roll or concentric circles), use Kernel PCA, t-SNE, or UMAP instead.
5.
Forgetting PCA Is Unsupervised: Maximum variance $\neq$ maximum class separability! Two parallel pancake-shaped classes may have their largest variance along the pancakes, while the direction separating them is actually PC2 (lowest variance). For supervised dimensionality reduction, consider Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA).
